School climate, belonging and student outcomes

An interactive guide to which parts of school life are linked to belonging, attendance and test scores

Based on Sims, S., Mills, B. and Oatley, S. Executive Summary: School Climate, Belonging and Student Outcomes. Nuffield Foundation. Read the summary (PDF).

Persistent absence roughly doubled in England and the US after the pandemic and is still well above pre-pandemic levels. One explanation is that the pandemic disrupted students’ relationships with each other and with their teachers, weakening their sense of belonging. If schools can improve their climate, the hope is that belonging rises, and attendance and test scores follow.

This project asks which aspects of school climate matter for which outcomes, using a unique panel of around 20,000 students a year in 45 Lift Schools across England, followed over two academic years (2024/25 and 2025/26).

45 schools (21 primary, 22 secondary, 2 special) ~20,000 students surveyed each year 86% / 90% student response 96% teacher response

1Five aspects of school climate

Climate is measured with the University of Chicago’s 5Essentials framework (Bryk et al., 2010). Two aspects are reported by students; three by teachers. Click one to see what it captures and how it was asked.

Teachers cannot be linked to individual students, so the three teacher-reported aspects are averaged to the school level. Their links with outcomes rest on differences between schools, and on changes in a school’s climate between the two years.

2The pathways being tested

The project expects climate to shape belonging, and belonging to shape attendance and test scores. Switch between what was hypothesised and the direct links found so far between each aspect of climate and each outcome.

3What we found

Each column is one model. Each cell is the change in that outcome linked to a one standard deviation (SD) rise in one aspect of climate, holding the other four fixed. Click a cell.

LargeModerateSmallVery smallNo consistent link

Three of the five aspects, Supportive Environment, Ambitious Teaching and Involved Families, are consistently linked to at least one outcome. Effective Leaders and Collaborative Teachers are not, once the others are held fixed (section 7).

The strongest analyses compare the same student across the two years: if a student’s view of their school improves, does their belonging or attendance improve too? This holds fixed many stable features of students. School belonging was only measured in 2025/26, so for that outcome students are compared within the same school in that year.

4How big is one standard deviation?

A one SD rise in an aspect of climate is roughly what you would see if half of respondents moved up one point on every question measuring it. For a student starting at the middle, it means moving from the 50th to the 84th percentile. Choose a relationship and move the slider.

5Which questions matter most?

Each aspect is measured by many survey items. Dominance analysis splits each model’s explained variance between the items. Each bar is an item’s share of the variance explained by the full model, not of all the variation in the outcome; the bars do not sum to 100% because the rest is explained by other aspects of climate, student characteristics and differences between schools.

6Support or challenge? Both.

A common worry is that demanding lessons come at the cost of students feeling they belong. The findings suggest otherwise: Supportive Environment and Ambitious Teaching are each linked to higher class belonging. Set where a student’s class sits on each.

Expected class belonging, from the class-belonging model
Class belonging percentile

Illustrative: combines the two published coefficients (+0.35 SD and +0.27 SD per SD) for a student at the middle of everything else, assuming each works additively and everything is normally distributed. It shows direction and rough scale, not a forecast for any one class.

Within Ambitious Teaching, what matters for class belonging is cognitive activation, lessons that make students think and feel they are learning a lot, not general statements of high expectations (“expects everyone to work hard” explained the least).

7Leadership and collaboration

Effective Leaders and Collaborative Teachers were associated with better outcomes when looked at on their own, but these associations disappeared once the other aspects of climate were taken into account.

Students directly experience safety, challenge and their parents’ involvement, but not how teachers collaborate or how leaders operate. In the Chicago research behind the 5Essentials, leadership drives the other essentials rather than acting on students directly. If so, models that hold the other four aspects fixed will understate leadership’s total contribution. The absence of a direct link is not evidence that leadership does not matter; the next stage tests these indirect pathways with structural equation modelling.

8What schools can do

Schools looking to improve belonging and engagement should prioritise safe school environments, trusting relationships with students and parents, and lessons that make students think hard.

1. Make school feel safe and respectful, starting with the spaces between lessons Supportive Environment

The strongest lever found for belonging, and the only aspect of climate linked to attendance.

  • Make corridors and shared spaces feel safe. Calm, orderly transitions between lessons may help, and may particularly benefit autistic students (Birkett et al., 2022). Anti-bullying programmes reduce victimisation (Gaffney et al., 2019).
  • Treat students with respect and keep promises. Respect was the strongest predictor of attendance; teachers doing what they say they will mattered for belonging. Fair, well-communicated, consistently applied behaviour policies support this.
  • Show students their views count. Feeling that the school involved them and valued their views was the single most important factor in school belonging.
2. Keep lessons challenging and support students to think hard Ambitious Teaching

There is no trade-off between rigour and belonging. Ask students to explain their answers, summarise their learning and consider different viewpoints, so they feel they are learning a lot. These lessons are linked to stronger class belonging and slightly higher test scores.

3. Build strong relationships with families Involved Families

Involving parents in school life and listening to their views is linked to higher test scores, and the link holds when the same students are compared over time, so it is not explained by stable family characteristics such as parents’ education. Approaches with some supporting evidence include teacher home visits and structured activities bringing families into school (Gamoran et al., 2012; Sheldon and Jung, 2018).

9Reading the results with caution

Associations, not causal relationships.

Comparing the same students over time rules out many non-causal explanations, but not all.

One trust.

Lift Schools are spread across England but serve a more disadvantaged population than average (around 36–43% eligible for free school meals against 28–33% nationally) and have somewhat lower absence. Several findings echo research elsewhere, but they may not apply to all schools.

Surveys show what matters, not how to change it.

Planned case studies of schools that have improved their climate will address this.

Some measures overlap in content.

Some Supportive Environment questions resemble belonging questions (e.g. “my teachers treat me with respect” and “people at this school are friendly to me”), which may inflate the link. Removing the two most similar items did not substantively change the results, but some overlap likely remains, so read the +0.67 SD figure with that in mind.

Indirect relationships may be understated.

Most plausibly for Effective Leaders (section 7).

Different belonging measures can give different results.

Other work using other belonging measures has found different links with attendance. These findings relate to the school- and class-belonging scales described below.

School belonging: six items from the Psychological Sense of School Membership scale (Goodenow, 1993), e.g. “I feel like a real part of my school” (α = .85; 2025/26 only). Class belonging: four items on the student’s English or maths class, e.g. “I feel like my classmates accept me for who I am” (α = .89 English, .88 maths).

10What happens next

  1. A third year of data (2026/27), strengthening within-student comparisons.
  2. Testing the pathways with structural equation modelling: does leadership work through a more supportive environment? Does class belonging explain the link between Ambitious Teaching and test scores?
  3. Ruling out further explanations by comparing the same student across subjects at the same time.
  4. Case studies in schools that have improved their climate, to give other schools a practical model.
